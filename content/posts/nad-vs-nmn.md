NAD vs NMN is the first question most people ask about NAD-boosting supplements. NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) helps cells turn food into energy and repair DNA. Because levels decline with age, NAD+ boosters have become popular "longevity" supplements. The question is which form actually works.

## Side-by-side

| | NAD+ (oral) | NMN | NR |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | The molecule itself | A direct precursor | Another precursor (a form of vitamin B3) |
| Raises blood NAD in human trials | Poorly studied orally | Yes | Yes |
| Typical trial dose | — | 250–900 mg/day | 300–1,000 mg/day |
| Proven anti-aging effects in humans | No | No | No |
| Cost | Moderate to high | High | High |

## Why oral NAD+ is a weak choice

NAD+ is a large molecule that's broken down in the gut and blood. Cells mostly build NAD+ from smaller precursors instead. That's why research focuses on NMN and NR.

## What the trials show

- **NMN:** a randomized trial in healthy middle-aged adults found NMN raised blood NAD levels in a dose-dependent way and was well tolerated over 60 days, with small improvements in some measures such as walking distance.
- **NR:** trials show raised NAD levels and good short-term safety. A trial in older adults with mild cognitive impairment did not show cognitive benefits.

Exciting animal results on lifespan and metabolism haven't translated into clear human benefits yet.

## Safety

Short-term studies report few side effects: occasional nausea, flushing or digestive upset. Long-term safety is unknown. Product purity varies, so choose third-party tested brands.

## The verdict

If you're going to try an NAD booster, **NMN or NR** make more sense than oral NAD+. But keep expectations realistic: human evidence for anti-aging benefits isn't there yet. Proven basics such as exercise, sleep and a good diet matter more for healthy aging.
