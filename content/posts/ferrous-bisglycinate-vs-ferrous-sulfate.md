Ferrous bisglycinate vs ferrous sulfate is the main choice most people face when buying iron. Iron supplements are essential when you're low, but many people quit because of stomach side effects. The form you choose can make a difference.

## Side-by-side

| | Ferrous bisglycinate | Ferrous sulfate |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Iron bound to two glycine molecules (chelate) | Iron salt |
| Typical elemental iron per tablet | 25–36 mg | 65 mg (in a 325 mg tablet) |
| Digestive side effects | Fewer in several trials | Common: constipation, nausea, cramps |
| Evidence | Growing; a 2023 meta-analysis | Decades; standard of care |
| Cost | Higher | Very low |

## What the research shows

A 2023 meta-analysis of randomized trials found that, in pregnant women, ferrous bisglycinate raised hemoglobin more and caused fewer gastrointestinal side effects than other iron salts, though the certainty of evidence was low. Other groups had less data.

Ferrous sulfate remains effective and is what most guidelines and studies use.

## Tips for any iron supplement

1. **Only take iron if a blood test shows you need it.** Too much iron is harmful, and iron overload conditions exist.
2. **Take it with vitamin C** (or orange juice) to boost absorption.
3. **Keep it apart from calcium, tea, coffee and antacids** by at least 2 hours.
4. **Separate it from levothyroxine and some antibiotics.** Check our [interaction checker](/tools/supplement-interaction-checker).
5. **Store iron away from children.** Iron overdose is dangerous for kids.

## The verdict

Start with whatever your clinician recommends, often ferrous sulfate. If it causes side effects that make you stop, **ferrous bisglycinate** is a gentler alternative with reasonable evidence. Pairing iron with vitamin C helps either form; see [how much vitamin C per day](/how-much-vitamin-c-per-day).
