Vitamin D deficiency is common, yet many people with it feel completely fine. That's what makes vitamin D deficiency symptoms tricky: by the time they're noticeable, levels have often been low for a long time.

## Common symptoms

When low vitamin D does cause symptoms, they tend to involve bones and muscles:

- **Bone pain or tenderness**, often in the lower back, hips, legs or ribs
- **Muscle weakness**, especially in the thighs and upper arms, making stairs or standing up harder
- **Muscle aches and cramps**
- **Fatigue**
- **Frequent falls** in older adults, partly because of muscle weakness

### In children

Severe deficiency causes **rickets**: soft, weak bones that lead to bowed legs, delayed growth, dental problems and skeletal deformities.

### In adults

Long-term deficiency causes **osteomalacia** (softening of the bones) and contributes to **osteoporosis**, raising the risk of fractures.

## What blood levels mean

Doctors measure 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D):

| Level | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Below 12 ng/mL (30 nmol/L) | Deficient |
| 12–20 ng/mL (30–50 nmol/L) | Possibly inadequate |
| 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) or higher | Adequate for most people |

Some labs and specialist groups use higher cut-offs, so ask how your result was interpreted.

## Who is most at risk

- **Little sun exposure:** indoor workers, people who cover most of their skin, long winters at higher latitudes
- **Darker skin**, which makes less vitamin D from the same sun
- **Adults over 65**, whose skin produces less vitamin D
- **Obesity**, as vitamin D is held in fat tissue
- **Breastfed infants** without a vitamin D supplement
- **Digestive conditions** that reduce fat absorption, such as Crohn's disease, celiac disease or weight-loss surgery
- **Kidney or liver disease**, which affect how vitamin D is activated
- **Certain medications**, including some anti-seizure drugs, glucocorticoids and orlistat

## Getting tested

If you have symptoms or several risk factors, ask your doctor about a 25(OH)D test. Routine screening of healthy adults without risk factors isn't generally recommended.

## How deficiency is treated

Treatment depends on how low you are. A doctor may prescribe a higher daily or weekly dose for a period, then a maintenance dose. Don't self-prescribe very high doses; vitamin D can build up to harmful levels. Read [how much vitamin D is too much](/how-much-vitamin-d-is-too-much) to understand the limits.

For prevention, most adults do well with 600–2,000 IU a day, taken with a meal that contains fat. See [the best time to take vitamin D](/best-time-to-take-vitamin-d).

## The bottom line

Vitamin D deficiency symptoms are often absent or vague: bone pain, muscle weakness and tiredness are the most typical. If you're in a higher-risk group or have these symptoms, a simple blood test gives a clear answer and guides the right dose.
