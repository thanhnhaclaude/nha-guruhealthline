Vitamin D3 and K2 are sold together so often that many people assume you *have* to take them as a pair. You don't, but for most people you *can*. Here's what each vitamin does, what the evidence actually supports, and when the combination isn't a good idea.

## What vitamin D and vitamin K2 each do

**Vitamin D** helps your gut absorb calcium and phosphorus. Without enough of it, bones can become thin and brittle. Your skin makes vitamin D from sunlight, and it's also found in fatty fish, egg yolks and fortified foods.

**Vitamin K** is needed to activate proteins that use calcium. Two of them matter here:

- **Osteocalcin**, which helps bind calcium into bone
- **Matrix Gla protein (MGP)**, which helps stop calcium from depositing in soft tissue such as artery walls

Vitamin K comes in two main forms. K1 (phylloquinone) is found in leafy greens. K2 (menaquinones such as MK-4 and MK-7) comes from fermented foods like natto and some cheeses, and from animal products.

## Why people pair them

The reasoning goes like this: vitamin D raises calcium absorption, and vitamin K2 activates the proteins that guide that calcium to bone and away from arteries. Taking both should get the best of each.

It's a sensible hypothesis. The problem is that the clinical evidence hasn't caught up:

- **Bone health:** Some trials, mainly from Japan using high-dose MK-4, found fewer fractures. Other trials with MK-7 found small or no effects on bone density. Results are mixed.
- **Arterial calcification:** Trials of vitamin K supplements on artery calcium scores have mostly shown no significant benefit.
- **Vitamin D "needing" K2:** There's no good human evidence that normal doses of vitamin D are harmful without K2.

So the combination is reasonable, but it isn't proven to do more than vitamin D alone.

## Typical doses

| | Common supplement dose | Official reference |
|---|---|---|
| Vitamin D3 | 600–2,000 IU (15–50 mcg) per day | RDA 600 IU for ages 1–70, 800 IU for 71+. Upper limit 4,000 IU (100 mcg) per day for adults |
| Vitamin K2 (MK-7) | 90–200 mcg per day | Adequate Intake for total vitamin K: 120 mcg (men), 90 mcg (women). No upper limit set |

Higher vitamin D doses are sometimes prescribed to correct a deficiency confirmed by a blood test. Those should be supervised by a clinician, not chosen on your own.

## When not to take vitamin K2

> **If you take warfarin (Coumadin, Jantoven) or another vitamin K antagonist, talk to your doctor before taking any vitamin K supplement.** Vitamin K directly counteracts these drugs. Even small, inconsistent changes in intake can shift your INR and raise the risk of clots.

Newer blood thinners such as apixaban and rivaroxaban work differently and aren't affected by vitamin K in the same way. Still, check with your prescriber before adding any supplement.

Also be cautious with:

- **High-dose vitamin D** if you have kidney disease, sarcoidosis or hyperparathyroidism, which can raise calcium levels
- **Long-term antibiotics** or fat malabsorption conditions, which can change vitamin K status. Ask your doctor before supplementing.

## How to take them together

- **Take them with a meal that contains fat.** Both vitamins are fat-soluble and absorb better with food. See [the best time to take vitamin D](/best-time-to-take-vitamin-d) for more.
- **Same time is fine.** There's no evidence that separating them improves anything.
- **Count every source.** Multivitamins, fortified foods and a D3+K2 combo can add up. Check that total vitamin D stays under 4,000 IU unless a doctor advises more.
- **Consider a blood test.** A 25-hydroxyvitamin D test tells you whether you actually need more vitamin D.

## The bottom line

Vitamin D3 and K2 are safe to take together for most healthy adults, and a combination product is a convenient option. Just know that K2's added benefit is still unproven, vitamin D alone at normal doses doesn't need a partner, and anyone on warfarin should leave vitamin K decisions to their doctor.
