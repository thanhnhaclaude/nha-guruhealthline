Collagen is the main structural protein in skin, tendons and bone. Collagen supplements come mostly from two sources: fish (marine) or cows (bovine). Here's how marine collagen vs bovine collagen compares.

## Side-by-side

| | Marine collagen | Bovine collagen |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Fish skin and scales | Cow hides and bones |
| Main collagen types | Type I | Types I and III |
| Suitable for | Pescatarians; people avoiding beef | Most omnivores |
| Allergy concern | Fish allergy | Beef allergy (rare) |
| Taste | Can be slightly fishy | Neutral |
| Cost | Usually higher | Usually lower |

## Does the source matter?

When you swallow collagen, it's broken down into amino acids and small peptides. Your body then builds its own collagen. A 2021 meta-analysis found that hydrolyzed collagen supplements improved skin hydration and elasticity compared with placebo, though the studies varied a lot and many were funded by manufacturers. Studies used both marine and bovine sources, and there isn't strong evidence that one is superior.

## Typical doses

Skin studies often used **2.5–10 g** of collagen peptides a day for 8–12 weeks. Collagen powder mixes easily into coffee, smoothies or water.

## Helpful extras

- **Vitamin C** is needed to make collagen. Most people get enough from food; see [how much vitamin C per day](/how-much-vitamin-c-per-day).
- **Protein intake overall** matters: collagen is an incomplete protein, so don't count on it as your main protein source.

## Quality checks

- Choose **third-party tested** products; contaminants such as heavy metals have been found in some collagen supplements.
- Check whether marine collagen is from **sustainably sourced** fish.

## The verdict

Marine and bovine collagen appear similarly effective. Pick **marine** if you avoid beef, **bovine** for a lower price and neutral taste, and give either 8–12 weeks before judging the results.
