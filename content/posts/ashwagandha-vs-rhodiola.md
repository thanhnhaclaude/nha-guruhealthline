Ashwagandha and rhodiola are both called **adaptogens**, herbs said to help the body cope with stress. They're often sold side by side, but they come from different traditions, have different research behind them, and suit different kinds of stress. This ashwagandha vs rhodiola comparison helps you match the herb to the problem.

## At a glance

| | Ashwagandha | Rhodiola |
|---|---|---|
| Plant | *Withania somnifera* (root) | *Rhodiola rosea* (root) |
| Tradition | Ayurvedic medicine, India | Folk medicine in Scandinavia, Russia and the Alps |
| Main use studied | Stress, anxiety scores, sleep | Mental and physical fatigue |
| Feels | Calming | Mildly energizing |
| Typical dose | 300–600 mg root extract per day | 200–600 mg extract per day |
| When to take | Evening or split morning/evening | Morning or early afternoon |
| Evidence strength | Moderate number of small trials | Fewer trials, mixed quality |

## What ashwagandha may help with

Ashwagandha has the larger body of human research of the two. In several randomized trials, adults taking root extract for 6–8 weeks reported lower stress and anxiety scores and, in some studies, lower cortisol than those taking placebo. A few trials also found modest improvements in sleep quality.

The catch: most studies are small, short, and some are funded by extract manufacturers. The NIH describes the evidence as promising but limited.

**It may suit you if:** stress keeps you on edge in the evening, you struggle to wind down, or your sleep is disrupted by worry.

## What rhodiola may help with

Rhodiola has mostly been studied for **fatigue**, both stress-related exhaustion and mental tiredness during demanding work. Some trials found less fatigue and better concentration; others found no clear effect. Overall the evidence is weaker and less consistent than for ashwagandha.

**It may suit you if:** you feel drained and foggy during the day, rather than anxious at night.

## Doses used in research

### Ashwagandha

- **300–600 mg per day** of a standardized root extract, often split into two doses
- Effects typically assessed after **6–8 weeks**
- Choose root extracts; some products include leaf, which contains different compounds

### Rhodiola

- **200–600 mg per day** of an extract standardized for rosavins and salidroside
- Usually taken in the **morning**, as it may interfere with sleep later in the day
- Start at the lower end and increase slowly if needed

## Side effects and who should avoid them

### Ashwagandha

- **Common:** drowsiness, upset stomach, loose stools
- **Rare but serious:** liver injury has been reported. Stop and see a doctor if you notice yellowing skin or eyes, dark urine or unusual fatigue.
- **Avoid if:** you're pregnant (it may raise miscarriage risk) or breastfeeding, have hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, or have a thyroid condition (it may raise thyroid hormone levels)
- **Check with your doctor if:** you take sedatives, thyroid medication, immunosuppressants, or drugs for diabetes or blood pressure, or you have an autoimmune disease

### Rhodiola

- **Common:** dizziness, dry mouth, trouble sleeping if taken late
- **Avoid if:** you're pregnant or breastfeeding, as safety hasn't been established
- **Check with your doctor if:** you take antidepressants, diabetes or blood pressure medication, or have bipolar disorder, since stimulating effects may be a concern

## Quality matters with herbs

Herbal products vary widely in strength and purity. To reduce risk:

- Choose **standardized extracts** that list active compounds
- Look for **third-party testing** (USP, NSF, ConsumerLab)
- Avoid proprietary blends that hide doses
- Be cautious with products promising dramatic results

## The verdict

If your stress shows up as tension and poor sleep, **ashwagandha** has the stronger evidence. If it shows up as daytime exhaustion and brain fog, **rhodiola** is the more fitting choice, with weaker evidence. If sleep is the main issue, simple steps such as a consistent bedtime or [checking your magnesium intake](/how-much-magnesium-per-day) are worth trying too. Neither herb replaces sleep, exercise or professional care for anxiety and depression, and both should be discussed with a doctor if you take medication or are pregnant.
