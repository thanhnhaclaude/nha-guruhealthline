Coenzyme Q10 sits inside your mitochondria, the parts of cells that turn food into energy. Organs with high energy needs, such as the heart, contain the most. Your body makes CoQ10, but levels fall with age and with some medications, which is why it's a popular supplement after 40.

## Statins and CoQ10

Statins block an enzyme that's needed to make both cholesterol and CoQ10, so they lower CoQ10 levels in the blood. That led to the idea that CoQ10 could ease statin-related muscle aches. The evidence is split: one meta-analysis found less muscle pain, while others found no clear benefit. If you get muscle symptoms on a statin, talk to your doctor first; there are other causes and other treatment options.

## Ubiquinone vs ubiquinol

| Form | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ubiquinone | Oxidized form, used in most research | Cheaper, well studied |
| Ubiquinol | Reduced (active) form | Often marketed as better absorbed; the advantage in practice is modest |

Both raise blood CoQ10 levels. Taking either with a meal that contains fat matters more than the form.

## How much to take

Most trials used **100–200 mg a day**, and some used up to 300 mg, often split into two doses. Doses above 100 mg are better tolerated when divided.

## Safety and interactions

CoQ10 is generally well tolerated. Possible side effects include upset stomach, nausea and insomnia at high doses.

- **Warfarin:** CoQ10 is chemically similar to vitamin K and may make warfarin less effective.
- **Blood pressure medicines:** CoQ10 may lower blood pressure slightly.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** safety isn't established.
