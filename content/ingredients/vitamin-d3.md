Your skin makes vitamin D when it's exposed to UVB sunlight, but latitude, season, sunscreen, skin tone and age all limit how much. Few foods contain much of it, so supplements are a common way to fill the gap.

## Who is most likely to need it

- People who get little direct sun, especially in winter at higher latitudes
- People with darker skin, which makes less vitamin D from the same sun exposure
- Adults over 70, whose skin makes less vitamin D
- People with conditions that affect fat absorption, such as Crohn's or celiac disease
- People with obesity, as vitamin D is stored in fat tissue
- Breastfed infants (pediatricians usually recommend drops)

## D3 vs D2

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) generally raises and maintains blood levels more effectively than D2 (ergocalciferol), so most supplements use D3. Vegan D3 made from lichen is available.

## How much to take

The RDA is 600 IU for most adults and 800 IU over 70. Many people take 1,000–2,000 IU a day. The upper limit is 4,000 IU. Higher doses to correct a deficiency should be prescribed and monitored. Take it with food for better absorption; the details are in [the best time to take vitamin D](/best-time-to-take-vitamin-d).

## Safety

Too much vitamin D raises blood calcium, which can cause nausea, weakness, kidney stones and heart rhythm problems. This almost always comes from high-dose supplements, not sun or food. Vitamin D can interact with thiazide diuretics, steroids and some weight-loss and cholesterol drugs. If you're considering K2 alongside it, read [whether you can take vitamin D and K2 together](/can-you-take-vitamin-d-and-k2-together).
