Vitamin B12 is found naturally only in animal foods such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy, plus fortified foods. Your body stores several years' worth in the liver, so deficiency develops slowly, and can be advanced before it's noticed.

## Who is most at risk

| Group | Why |
|---|---|
| Vegans and many vegetarians | Little or no B12 in the diet |
| Adults over 50 | Less stomach acid to release B12 from food |
| Metformin users | Long-term use lowers B12 absorption |
| Long-term PPIs or H2 blockers | Reduced stomach acid |
| Pernicious anemia | The body can't make intrinsic factor needed to absorb B12 |
| Gut surgery or Crohn's disease | Reduced absorption |

The NIH advises adults over 50 to get most of their B12 from supplements or fortified foods, which are absorbed more easily.

## Symptoms of deficiency

- Tiredness, weakness and shortness of breath (from anemia)
- Numbness or tingling in hands and feet
- Problems with balance
- Memory problems, confusion or low mood
- Sore, smooth tongue

Nerve damage from long-term deficiency can become permanent, so don't ignore these symptoms. A blood test confirms it.

## Forms and doses

- **Cyanocobalamin:** stable, inexpensive and well studied.
- **Methylcobalamin:** an active form; works well but has no proven advantage for most people.

Because only a small portion of a large oral dose is absorbed, supplements often contain **25–1,000 mcg**. High oral doses can correct deficiency even in many people with absorption problems. Injections are used for severe deficiency or pernicious anemia.

## Safety

No upper limit has been set; excess is excreted. B12 can interact with some medicines: metformin, PPIs, H2 blockers and chloramphenicol may lower levels. Our [dose calculator](/tools/supplement-dose-calculator) shows the daily target for your age.
